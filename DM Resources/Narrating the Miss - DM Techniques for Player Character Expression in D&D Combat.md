# Narrating the Miss: DM Techniques for Player Character Expression in D&D Combat

## TL;DR

* The technique works and is widely endorsed. Just as Matt Mercer's "How do you want to do this?" hands players the killing blow, a DM can hand players the defense — asking "how does your character avoid/absorb that?" after a miss (especially after 2+ consecutive misses against a high-AC character) to spotlight defensive class fantasy. It costs almost nothing mechanically and turns dead-air misses into character moments.
* The main tradeoff is pacing and player comfort. Combat already runs slowly, and some players get decision paralysis when put on the spot, while others revel in every chance to role-play. The consensus fix is to gate the prompt behind trigger conditions (only after multiple misses, only on a killing blow, only once per round/session) and to scale participation to each player's comfort.
* This fills a real gap. Most published spotlight-balance advice targets social/exploration scenes; combat-specific spotlight tools are thinner, which is exactly why player-narrated hits, misses, spells, saves, and heals are valuable — they distribute narrative authority around the table during the game's most mechanically dominant mode.

Sourcing note: The specific Reddit post by user "SixFootFox" described in the task could not be located or verified through web search or a dedicated follow-up search. The technique itself and the community debate around it are well-attested across professional DM blogs (The Angry GM, ThinkDM, Sly Flourish), Critical Role commentary, and D&D forums (D&D Beyond, EN World), which this report draws on. Where the alleged top comment's "class fantasy" phrasing appears below, it is treated as representative community sentiment, not a verified quote.

## Key Findings

1. The "narrate the kill" convention already exists and is beloved. Matt Mercer's "How do you want to do this?" (HDYWTDT) is the canonical example, uttered when a player lands a killing blow on a significant enemy so the player can describe the finish. Per the Critical Role wiki, the phrase "organically formed during the campaign previous to Vox Machina's, around 2009–2010," and Mercer has used it publicly ever since Critical Role began streaming in March 2015. The "narrate the miss/dodge/block" idea is the logical inversion: give players narrative authority over defense, not just offense.
2. Professional DMs already narrate misses by "line of defense." ThinkDM's "Narrating Missed Attacks" (June 29, 2019) builds an order-of-operations based on how close the miss was: a roll under ~10 is a wild swing/operator error; then a shield block; then a dodge (the Dexterity contribution); then a glance off armor. ThinkDM's own insight is that a shield "isn't your last line of defense — it's one of your first," so a miss "by 2 or less" against a shielded target reads best as hitting the shield. The Angry GM's "Barking at Your Players: Advanced Combat Narration" gives the parallel rule for monsters: "if the monster is nimble and lightly armored, describe misses as dodges. If the monster is heavily armored, describe misses as bouncing off its shell." The same logic maps cleanly onto PCs (agile monk = dodge; plate-clad paladin = deflection).
3. There is a real spectrum of player appetite for improvised RP. The Angry GM notes some players won't describe anything ("I attack, 17") while others turn every swing into a performance — warning that "Every attack becomes a damned Shakespearian soliloquy. And it gets old. Fast." Decision paralysis / analysis paralysis is a well-documented table phenomenon. The technique must therefore be opt-in-able and gently scaled.
4. A whole family of adjacent techniques exists — narrating the kill (HDYWTDT), narrating misses/dodges, spell flavor ("describe how you cast it"), fail-forward ("failing with style"), narrating critical hits taken, class-flavor prompts ("why do you rage?"), and Colville's action-oriented "villain actions" that give the DM cinematic beats. Together these distribute expressive moments across offense, defense, magic, and failure.
5. Combat-specific spotlight advice is comparatively underdeveloped. Most spotlight-balance guidance addresses social scenes. Player-narrated defense is a lightweight way to give non-active players (those whose turn it isn't) a moment when they're targeted.

## Details

### Technique 1: "How do you avoid that?" — Player-narrated defense

Description. When an attack misses a PC — most powerfully when 2+ attacks miss in a row, or a big attack whiffs against a high-AC character — the DM turns to the player and asks them to describe how they avoid or absorb it. This mirrors HDYWTDT but for defense. A high-AC paladin narrates standing firm as blows ring off plate; a monk describes flowing out of the way; a duelist parries and ripostes.

Why it works. It converts "you miss" (dead air) into a character beat, and it visualizes AC — otherwise an abstract number. It is especially valuable for tank/defensive builds, whose class fantasy (being hard to hit or hard to hurt) is rarely spotlighted because "nothing happened" on a miss. It lets a player lean into class fantasy: are they ducking and weaving, letting blows rain ineffectually off their armor, or parrying and slipping counters through the enemy's defense? ThinkDM's framework gives DMs a mental model for which flavor fits a given miss — how badly the roll missed AC maps to dodge vs. armor-deflection vs. shield-block.

Tradeoffs / debate.

* Pacing: Combat is already slow; doing this every miss would grind it to a halt. The Angry GM explicitly warns about over-narration.
* Spotlight anxiety: Being asked to improvise on the spot can freeze decision-paralysis-prone players.
* Metagaming leak: ThinkDM cautions that if you narrate misses too precisely (shield vs. dodge vs. armor), players can reverse-engineer a target's AC — keep PC defense narration loose so it doesn't broadcast exact numbers.

Implementation tips.

* Trigger conditions: Reserve it for high-impact moments — 2+ consecutive misses on one PC, a natural-1 enemy attack, a boss's big attack that whiffs, or the first miss in a fight. A common self-imposed cap is "no more than once per round" or "once per character per combat."
* Sample prompts: "That's the third arrow to miss you — what does that look like?" / "The ogre's club comes down hard and… 8, that bounces off your shield. How do you shrug it off?" / "It rolls a 4 against your 19 AC. You barely seem to notice — paint the picture."
* Scale by comfort: For enthusiastic players, open-ended prompts. For hesitant players, offer a menu ("Do you parry it, sidestep, or let it glance off your armor?") — multiple-choice defeats decision paralysis. For the shyest, the DM narrates for them and just gets a nod, occasionally lobbing an easy prompt when momentum is high.

### Technique 2: "How do you want to do this?" — Player-narrated kills

Description. When a PC drops a significant enemy to 0 HP, the DM lets the player narrate the finishing blow. Origin: Matt Mercer / Critical Role, where it became a signature ("HDYWTDT"). Variants: "How do you want to kill it?", Sam Riegel's "How would you like to end its life?", and Mercer turning it on himself — "How do I want to do this?" — in Campaign 2, Episode 87 ("Punishment and Politics"), when his NPC Jourrael landed the final blow on Obann the Punished.

Why it works. Outcome is already decided (dead is dead), so handing narration to the player is pure upside — the safest possible moment to cede authority.

Tradeoffs. Forum DMs (D&D Beyond, EN World) note two frictions: (1) some feel it is Mercer's "trademark" and want their own phrasing; (2) some players give flat answers ("I cut off its head"), requiring the DM to elaborate for dramatic effect. Reserve it for meaningful kills (bosses, minibosses, personal nemeses), not every goblin, or it loses impact.

### Technique 3: Spell-casting flavor — "describe how you cast it"

Description. Players narrate the sensory character of their magic rather than just naming the spell. Community examples (D&D Beyond "Flavouring spells" threads): False Life pulling screaming green souls into the caster; Misty Step as skeletal shadow-arms dragging the caster away; bards weaving somatic/material components into performance.

Why it works. Differentiates same-spell casters, reinforces subclass/character identity, and adds table color. Tips: Don't demand it every cast (it slows things down and gets old); invite it on first casts, signature spells, or dramatic beats. A good starter prompt: "How does that Fireball look coming out of your hands?"

### Technique 4: Fail-forward / "failing with style"

Description. A failed roll still moves the story, with a complication. DMDavid ("How to Avoid Boring Rerolls of D&D's Ability Checks") ties this to the 5e rule (DMG p. 242 / Basic Rules) that failure can mean a character "makes progress combined with a setback determined by the DM" — e.g., "if a rogue blows a roll to pick a lock, the DM can decide that the lock opens but the attempt made a noise that leads to complications. Gamers call this failing forward." In combat, DMs narrate crit-fails/misses with color ("nothing but air," "the monster laughs," "you wipe dust from your eyes") rather than a bare "you miss."

Why it works. Failure becomes character-revealing and tension-building rather than a dead stop. Debate: Critics (Points of Light, "The Failings of Fail Forward") argue overzealous fail-forward ("you succeed, but…") can make the dice feel meaningless; keep genuine failure on the table and use narration to make it interesting, not to erase it.

### Technique 5: Class-flavor prompts ("how does your rage look?")

Description. Prompts that ask players to define the sensory/emotional signature of a class feature — e.g., barbarian rage as hot fury, cold precision, or a boxer's hype-up (D&D Beyond and EN World "Barbarian, Why Do You Rage?" threads). Applies equally to a monk's ki, a paladin's smite glow, a warlock's patron manifestation, etc.

Why it works. Turns a mechanical toggle into an identity marker. Best deployed at first use in a campaign, then referenced consistently rather than re-prompted every round.

### Technique 6: DM-side cinematic beats — Colville's action-oriented monsters

Description. Matt Colville introduced "action-oriented monsters" in a 2019 YouTube video (later formalized in MCDM's Flee, Mortals!, PDF released July 31, 2023; hardcover Sept. 1, 2023 — a 393-page book with 339 stat blocks whose Kickstarter raised $2,084,118 from 27,009 backers). These bosses get "villain actions" — legendary-action-like moves keyed to rounds. Sly Flourish summarizes the improvisational arc as Position → Escape → Explode, plus boosted HP.

Why it (indirectly) supports player expression. By giving the DM structured, dramatic beats, it raises the theatrical stakes of a fight, inviting players to match that energy with their own narration. It also pairs with HP-shifting: Sly Flourish advises shaving a monster's HP so a beaten-up player lands the satisfying kill, or "minionizing" a monster so it drops next hit — which manufactures more HDYWTDT moments.

## The pacing-vs-immersion balance (the core practical tension)

Every source that praises narration also flags the cost. The workable synthesis:

1. Gate it. Use trigger conditions so narration is occasional spice, not a per-roll tax.
2. Narrate after resolution. The Angry GM: resolve the roll, then narrate, so description reflects the actual outcome and doesn't stall the mechanic.
3. Set a floor, not a ceiling, for declarations. Always require target + method ("I attack the goblin with my sword"); invite flourish only sometimes.
4. Keep it loose to avoid metagaming (ThinkDM).

## Scaling participation by player comfort (spotlight without spotlight anxiety)

* The eager RPer: open-ended prompts; let them run, but cap length if they soliloquize.
* The decision-paralysis player: offer a 2–3 option menu; or spotlight them only when the moment is easy and high-energy; use an "unspoken agreement" to occasionally invite them without making it a recurring demand they dread.
* The reluctant player: DM narrates for them; accept a one-word buy-in; never force a monologue. The goal is inclusion, not exposure.

## Recommendations

Stage 1 — Start small (next 1–2 sessions). Adopt HDYWTDT for boss/miniboss kills only. Lowest-risk, highest-reward entry point; it trains the table that players sometimes hold the narrative pen.

Stage 2 — Add player-narrated defense with a hard trigger. Introduce "how do you avoid that?" but only after 2+ consecutive misses on one PC, or a boss's big whiffed attack, and at most once per round. Pre-brief the table: "Sometimes when you dodge something dramatic, I'll ask you to describe it — totally fine to pass or have me do it."

Stage 3 — Layer in spell flavor and fail-forward on signature moments (first casts, crit-fails), plus a one-time class-flavor prompt per PC ("show me what your rage/smite/ki looks like") that you then reuse.

Stage 4 — Upgrade the DM side. Build one action-oriented boss with Position/Escape/Explode villain actions to raise the theatrical ceiling, and use HP-shifting to engineer satisfying finishing blows for players who have been unlucky.

Benchmarks that should change your approach:

* If combat is dragging (rounds taking >10–15 min, players disengaging): tighten triggers — kills only, drop per-miss narration.
* If a specific player freezes repeatedly: switch them to menu prompts or narrate for them; stop cold-calling.
* If players start metagaming AC from your miss descriptions: deliberately vary and loosen your narration.
* If the table is hungry for more: loosen triggers, invite defense narration on first misses, and let players narrate their own critical hits and near-death saves.

It could not be located; the "lean into class fantasy / ducking and weaving / rain down on your armor / parrying their strikes" phrasing is treated here as representative community sentiment, not a confirmed quote. If precise attribution matters, locate the post independently before citing it.
* Much supporting evidence is craft advice, not empirical study. Blogs (Angry GM, ThinkDM, Sly Flourish, DMDavid) and forum threads reflect experienced-DM consensus, not research. Treat them as best-practice heuristics.
* Search-engine noise was significant for these terms (video-game "dodge/AC/parry" content dominated results), so niche Reddit threads on this exact technique may exist but were not surfaced.
* Table variance is real. These techniques are strongly table- and player-dependent; the same prompt that delights one group will stall another. Calibrate, don't apply mechanically.
